# Stage 1 pitches (2026-11-15)

Three odd true stories. Each has a first line, the mocker, the twist, a title that hides the ending, its places and its kinds of light. Facts were checked on 2026-11-15. Anything that is legend is marked and will be told as one.

---

## A. "The Man Who Sold Bed Warmers to the Tropics"
**Hero:** Timothy Dexter, Newburyport, Massachusetts, 1790s. A self-made "Lord".
**First line (13 words):** A man in Massachusetts loads a whole ship with bed warmers and sends it to the Caribbean.
**Mocker:** The harbour merchants, laughing at the dock: "Why not send coal to Newcastle while you're at it?" Dexter just nods and writes it down.
**Escalation in threes:** bed warmers to the tropics, then coal to a coal town, then he builds a garden of wooden statues of great men, with himself among them.
**"You see,"** the islands boil sugar into molasses all day, and a long-handled brass pan is exactly what you need to scoop it, like using a spoon for something it was never made for.
**Twist / last line (loop):** He writes a book about it with no punctuation at all, and on the very last page he prints a page of nothing but commas and full stops, so readers can "pepper and salt it" as they please.
**Places (7):** Newburyport wharf, merchants' tavern, ship deck at sea, Caribbean boiling house, Newcastle coal quay, Dexter's mansion with its statue garden, a printer's shop.
**Light (3):** cold harbour morning, hard tropical noon in steam, candle-lit print shop at night.
**Honesty:** Documented: the self-styled "Lord" title, his own boast of shipping 42,000 warming pans at 79 % profit, the statues, the punctuation page. Legend, told as "the story goes": the molasses ladles and coal to Newcastle. No customs records exist for these (W. C. Todd's inquiry).
**Sources:** American National Biography (Dexter, 1747-1806); W. C. Todd, *Timothy Dexter: An Inquiry into His Life and True Character*; *A Pickle for the Knowing Ones* (Project Gutenberg 43453); Public Domain Review; Museum of Old Newbury.

## B. "The Chicken That Skipped Dinner"
**Hero:** Mike, a rooster, Fruita, Colorado, 1945. With the Olsen family.
**First line (14 words):** A farmer in Colorado drips milk with an eyedropper straight down his chicken's neck, because the chicken has no head.
**Mocker:** A neighbour leaning on the fence: "You're feeding a chicken with no face? He's dinner, he just doesn't know it." The Olsens stay calm and keep feeding.
**Escalation in threes:** Mike still struts the yard, then a university checks him and can't fault it, then crowds pay a quarter each to see him on tour.
**"You see,"** the axe missed the big vein and left the bottom of the brain, the part that runs breathing and the heartbeat, like how your legs keep walking while your head thinks about something else.
**Twist / last line (loop):** Mike lived another eighteen months, and the town that was supposed to eat him now throws him a party every single year.
**Places (7):** chopping block in the farmyard, the Olsens' kitchen, the fence line with the neighbour, a university lab in Salt Lake City, a sideshow tent on tour, a magazine photo studio, today's Fruita festival street.
**Light (3):** low Colorado autumn sun, warm tungsten sideshow, festival afternoon with string lights.
**Honesty:** Documented: September 10, 1945, LIFE magazine (October 22, 1945), touring sideshow at 25 cents, about 18 months, Fruita festival since 1999. Not used or kept soft: the $4,500 a month figure (unverified) and the exact way he died. LIFE credits Clara Olsen with the axe, so the script will say "the Olsens".
**Sources:** LIFE retrospective (life.com); miketheheadlesschicken.org (family history); Los Angeles Times 2009; Rocky Mountain PBS 2023; Smithsonian.

## C. "The Private Who Was Paid in Beer"
**Hero:** Wojtek, a Syrian brown bear, 22nd Artillery Supply Company, Polish II Corps, 1942-1947.
**First line (12 words):** A soldier walks up the gangplank of a troop ship, and he's a bear.
**Mocker:** The British port officer, with a clipboard: "No animals on my ship." The Polish sergeant just hands over a paybook with the bear's name in it.
**Escalation in threes:** a cub traded for a few tins of food in Iran, then a camp bear who wrestles soldiers and drinks beer, then he catches a hiding man in a shower hut in Iraq.
**"You see,"** a soldier can go anywhere the army sends him, and an animal can't, like how a ticket gets you on a train but a note from home doesn't.
**Twist / last line (loop):** After the war the company changed its badge, and to this day it shows a bear carrying an artillery shell.
**Places (7):** mountain road in Iran, desert camp in Iraq with the shower hut, Egyptian port and the troop ship, ship deck at sea, Italian supply line under Monte Cassino, ammunition truck, Edinburgh Zoo.
**Light (3):** dusty desert noon, artillery-lit night at Cassino, grey Scottish daylight.
**Honesty:** Documented: Iran 1942, officially entered in the company's records, the bear-with-shell emblem, Edinburgh Zoo until 1963, statues in Edinburgh and Krakow. Soldiers' stories, told as such: beer and eating cigarettes, the shower-hut spy. Disputed, so it is the company's legend and not claimed as fact: carrying shells at Cassino (an officer's memoir doubts it).
**Sources:** Institute of National Remembrance (IPN); Imperial War Museums memorial register 78065; British Library "Wojtek, the soldier bear"; Hoover Institution "Wojtek, the Bear of Monte Cassino"; krakow.pl.

---

### Recommendation
**B (Mike)** has the strongest mocker-to-payoff arc. It has one hero, a simple cast (farmer, wife, neighbour, scientist, crowd) and a clean loop (dinner, then the town party). **A** has the funniest escalation and the most places. **C** has the biggest world and the scale shots, but it is the most retold story online.
